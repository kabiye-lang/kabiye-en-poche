Curriculum proposal · revised 7 September 2026

Zero to fluent in Kabiyè

A five-level course of 235 lessons in 39 units, built from every source we hold and ordered by how often words actually occur. Each lesson names where it was learned from; every one was checked against the lexicon before it went on the map.

Now 18 units · 78 lessons · one flat tier Proposed 5 levels · 39 units · 235 lessons · ~2,040 words Sources every document we hold — 4 courses, 1 dictionary, 9 texts, 3 studies

The question that reframed this

After 51 lessons, can they speak?

No. They can survive.

Fifty-one lessons at six words each is about 300 words — 59% of running text. Enough to greet, count, buy something, ask the way, and say where it hurts. Not enough to follow a story, argue a point, or read a poem. That is a solid A2, and it is a foundation, not a destination.

The grammar makes the gap plainer than the vocabulary does. The SIL sketch documents ten noun classes with ten matching determiner forms, eight singular/plural genders, nine categories of pronoun, and six conjugation schemas covering some twenty verb sub-classes across the descriptive, progressive, past, simple, accomplished and consecutive aspects. My 51 lessons allocated nine slots to all of that. Mastery needs closer to forty.

The shape

Five levels, each a real threshold

Levels are set by what share of ordinary Kabiyè a learner can follow at that point — a measurable line, not a feeling. The specialist lessons cut from the first draft are not gone; they move to B1, where a learner has the grammar to use them.

FoundationA1 · A2

Writing and sound, the sentence spine, greetings, numbers and time, everyday verbs, home, food, the body, work, travel, shopping, nature, and a first taste of culture. Eight units: sound, the sentence spine, people, numbers, first verbs, home and market, out and about, culture — then eight more that add noun classes, pronouns, the first verb forms, conversation skills and a first folktale.

The ordering principle, unchanged

Frequency decides the sequence at every level

The current curriculum teaches four of the hundred most frequent words. These are the ones a learner meets in every sentence they will ever read, and none of them appears in any lesson today. They open the course.

What the sources can carry

What each source contributes

Every document we hold is in the process now. The free Peace Corps workbook is used in full — its sentences attest, its glossary added 180 words the dictionary lacked, its lessons give ours their shape. The three L'Harmattan books are learned from: their grammar rules ground the generator, their paradigms let the validator accept conjugated verbs, their sequence and vocabulary order ours.

Its thirteen lessons are named as things a learner can do, not topics: Ask for and give direction and time. Express one's sympathy in a happy or sad event. Ask for help in an emergency case. That naming is better than ours, and free to adopt.

Learned from, never reproduced. The three L'Harmattan books are personally licensed to the project. We take their structure and their facts about the language — sequence, rules, paradigms, tone schemes, which words a lesson exercises — and write every lesson ourselves from the attested lexicon. Their sentences are dropped at extraction, so the generator never sees them. The corpus gate enforces the same line: their text is not mined.

Why the community is not a later feature

Two problems only speakers can solve

A forum reads like a growth idea. For this project it is closer to infrastructure, because it is the only realistic answer to the two things that cap the course.

  • Audio. No lesson has any. Teaching a tone language silently is the largest unmanaged risk in the project, and roughly 1,100 recordings are needed. That is not a budget problem, it is a people problem — and a community of speakers is the supply.
  • The long tail. Past about 2,300 words the dictionary thins out: only 2,294 lexemes carry an example sentence, so B2 and C1 content degrades exactly where it needs to be richest. Native speakers supply examples, dialect variants and corrections at a rate no pipeline can.

Both argue for shipping a contribution path before the advanced levels — report a word, add an example, record a reading — so that by the time C1 is being written, the material to write it from exists. C1 then produces the contributors who feed it, which is the only version of this that compounds.

Before you commit

What I'd want argued with

  • 300 lessons is a five-year artefact, not a sprint. A1–A2 is 105 lessons and reachable now. B1 upward depends on rights, audio and community. Sequencing the build matters as much as sequencing the course.
  • “Exhaustive” has a ceiling worth naming. 6,418 words in the dictionary are teachable at all; one lesson per six of them is 1,069 lessons, which is a dictionary with a progress bar. ~2,700 words at 91% coverage is where I would stop.
  • Verified, not assumed. An earlier draft of this page said the dictionary lacked wɛʋ, the existential “to be”. It does not — the entry is there from both sources, “être / exister” — and the first generated Being and Having teaches it. The claim came from a truncated listing; the lesson corrected it.
  • The workbook's encoding is broken — it writes ʋ as Greek upsilon and mislabels its own alphabet page “the lama alphabet”. Usable, but it needs normalising, not trusting.
  • Level boundaries are mine, not a standard. CEFR was not written for a Gur language with ten noun classes. The percentages are real; the labels are a convenience.
Timing still holds. 67 of the current 78 lessons have never been generated. Restructuring first costs nothing; restructuring after a full run means paying for it twice.
